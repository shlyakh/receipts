Receipts for forecasting papers · a transfer test · 13 Nov 2026

A model and a regression tied in Britain. Do they tie on a US grid?

A study published this week found that a pretrained forecasting model and a plain regression roughly tie on Great Britain's electricity demand once both get the same weather. We asked the same question of two zones of PJM, a US grid operator, forecasting a full week ahead.

Background

Electricity demand is forecast before the power is made.

Power cannot be stored cheaply at grid scale, so supply must match demand every hour. Grid operators decide days ahead which power plants will run. They decide from a forecast of demand, called load.

Seven regional operators run the wholesale grid for most of the United States. PJM is the largest, with about 65 million people across 13 states and Washington DC.

A forecast that is too low means expensive last-minute power. One that is too high means plants paid to stand by.

What this establishes

Load forecasts decide which plants are scheduled, days ahead. A few percent of error is real money, which is why each new forecasting model gets attention.

The benchmark

Two PJM zones that behave differently.

PJM is split into about twenty delivery zones. The benchmark forecasts two of them, one hour at a time, a full week ahead. JC is Jersey Central in New Jersey: residential, and driven by summer heat. DOM is Dominion in Virginia: large and growing, with heavy data-centre demand.

Temperature is the main driver in both, so every method gets the same temperature series: the average of three weather stations per zone. Both zones have public hourly data back to 2019 and sit in different climates 400 km apart.

Try this

Switch the bars to "Multiple of own average". In megawatts DOM dwarfs JC. Measured against its own average, JC's peak is the larger of the two. A method that wins in one zone and not the other has a win that depends on the zone.

The methods

Seven methods, the same history and the same temperature.

Every Monday at midnight each method forecasts the next 168 hours. Nothing after that moment may be used. A week ahead is hard because the weather forecast gets worse each day out, and because holidays and heat waves break the weekly rhythm.

Error is the average gap in megawatts between forecast and measured load over the 168 hours, averaged over 48 test weeks. Two methods are compared by dividing one error by the other. A ratio of 0.73 means 27% less error than the regression with temperature.

What this establishes

Every score in this story is a ratio to one baseline, the regression with temperature, on the same weeks and the same inputs. The baseline was named before any scoring.

The receipt

The tie does not transfer to DOM and is undecided on JC.

The Great Britain study forecast one day ahead over 960 days. With the same weather given to both, Chronos-2's error was 0.966 of the regression's, and the 90% range reached 1.

The rule for reading our result was fixed before scoring. If the whole 90% range sits below 0.90, the model is worth switching to. If the whole range sits between 0.90 and 1.10, the two are a practical tie and the claim transfers. Anything else is undecided.

Keep the two studies separate

This is a transfer test and says nothing about whether the Great Britain result is correct. That study is day ahead, and its regression also had a 2-day lag, wind and solar. Ours is a week ahead with a 7-day lag and temperature only. Our regression is also not the strongest simple method: LightGBM on the same inputs scores 0.857 on JC and 0.947 on DOM.

Inside the week

The lead is largest on day 1, and it depends on temperature.

The week-ahead ratio averages seven forecast days. Day 1 is the closest match to the Great Britain study's day-ahead setting, and there the model leads in both zones. On JC the ranges for days 3 to 5 include 1.

Two checks test where the lead comes from. In the scored run the model sees load up to the cutoff, while real load data arrives 1 to 2 days late, so one row withholds the last 48 hours. Another row gives Chronos-2 no weather at all.

What this establishes

A realistic data delay shrinks the day-1 lead and leaves the week-ahead verdicts unchanged. Without temperature, Chronos-2 is worse than the regression with temperature on DOM and no better on JC. Its lead comes from using temperature well, more than from the load history alone.

Out-of-sample check

Timestral has the lowest error and one unseen week.

A pretrained model may have been trained on the public series it is later tested on. The check is a date: only weeks that start after the model's weights were published count as unseen. Earlier years of the same series may still be in the training data of any of these models.

Timestral with temperature has the lowest error on the board in every zone and weather setting. Its only weights file is dated 15 Oct 2026, after all but one of the 48 scored weeks began. The Great Britain study also treats Timestral as having no usable unseen backtest.

What this establishes

Timestral's score cannot be told apart from leakage with this window, so it is shown and marked as not out-of-sample. The ratio on weeks after the weights would rest on a single week and is not reported.

Conclusion

What the evidence supports.

Under a protocol frozen before scoring, the rough tie reported for Great Britain does not transfer to DOM, where Chronos-2 with temperature clears the 10% bar. On JC the range crosses the bar, so 48 weeks cannot separate a tie from a real difference.

The result has limits. The model saw load up to the cutoff, the regression has a 7-day lag and temperature only, LightGBM on the same inputs is closer to the model than the regression is, and one year of weekly origins gives wide ranges.

Would the tie return against a stronger simple method, or on the other PJM zones?

Not done yet, as listed in the results:

  • A fair test of Timestral and other models whose weights fall inside the scored window.
  • A test of TimesFM 2.5 with a curved temperature term in place of the straight line.
  • Runs of other pretrained models on the same 48 weeks.
